Workers' comp audit and uninsured subcontractors: what it costs and how to avoid it

Updated November 2026. Written for US construction and property firms.

Your workers' comp insurer checks your books after the policy year ends. If you paid a subcontractor and can't show their own workers' comp certificate, that money can be treated as your payroll. You can estimate the charge with the calculator below and use the checklist to get ready.

What is a workers' comp premium audit?

Your workers' comp premium is first set from an estimate of your payroll. After the policy year ends, the insurer audits your real figures and recalculates the premium. The auditor looks at payroll, at the type of work your people did and at what you paid subcontractors. You then get a refund or an extra bill.

The audit is routine, but it's where missing paperwork turns into money, because the auditor works from what you can show, not from what you remember.

Why does a lapsed certificate become my problem?

A certificate is proof a sub had insurance on the day you collected it. The policy can be cancelled or run out afterwards, and the paper in your file goes stale. If that sub then causes damage or someone gets hurt, the claim can land on your own policy and you carry the liability.

Missing certificates cost money at audit. Once a year your insurer runs a premium audit. That's the check where they recount your payroll and what you paid subs. If a sub had no workers' comp and you have no current certificate on file, carriers add what you paid that sub to your own premium. The construction insurer BITCO says so on its premium audit page. Why do uninsured subcontractors raise my workers' comp premium?

At your workers' comp audit, the insurer treats payments to subs who lacked their own coverage as payroll to employees and charges premium on it. Keeping valid COIs with workers' comp on file for every sub is what prevents that surcharge.

New York's state insurance fund puts it plainly on its subcontractor coverage page: "If during your audit, you cannot produce Workers' Compensation insurance coverage documents for your subcontractors, your policy premium will be recalculated to include them in your payroll." The same page says you'll be asked to produce all subcontractor certificates of insurance during the audit.

That's New York's rule. NYSIF says it follows guidelines set by the New York Compensation Insurance Rating Board for subcontractor charges. Other states and carriers have their own rules, so ask your broker how yours works. The construction insurer BITCO describes the same approach on its premium audit page.

How much could an uninsured subcontractor add to my premium?

Roughly, what you paid the sub, times your class rate per $100 of payroll, divided by 100, times your experience modifier. That's the general premium formula published by SAIF, Oregon's state workers' comp insurer. Enter your own figures in the calculator below to get an estimate for your business.

How the estimate is worked out

SAIF's premium and rate overview gives the general formula: "Multiply your payroll by your class rate and divide by 100", then "Multiply that number by your experience rating modifier, if applicable". The calculator does exactly that, with your payments to undocumented subs in place of payroll.

Example with made-up numbers: you paid an uninsured framing sub $80,000. Your class rate for that work is 8.50 per $100 of payroll and your experience modifier is 1.00. The estimate is $80,000 times 8.50, divided by 100, times 1.00, which is $6,800. These figures are for illustration only. They aren't real rates for any state.

SAIF's formula also has further steps for other factors and premium discounts, which the calculator leaves out. Treat the result as a rough estimate. Your carrier, your state's rules and the auditor's view of the work decide the real figure.

Where do I find my class rate and experience modifier?

Look at your workers' comp policy's information page, sometimes called the declarations page. It lists each class code for your work, the rate charged per $100 of payroll for that code, and your experience modifier if you have one. If you can't find them, your insurance agent can read them to you in a minute.

SAIF's overview says the same: "Refer to your policy information page for rating factors specific to calculating your premium." Use the class code that matches the work the sub did on your job. If the sub did several kinds of work, ask your agent which code an auditor would use.

What does a workers' comp audit ask for about subcontractors?

Expect the auditor to ask which subs you paid during the policy year, how much you paid each one, and for a certificate of insurance showing each sub's own workers' comp for the dates they worked. Ask your carrier for its exact list before the audit, because carriers and states differ.

Use this as a printable checklist before the auditor arrives.

  1. A list of every subcontractor you paid during the policy year, with their legal business names.
  2. The total you paid each one in that year, matching your books.
  3. For each sub, a certificate of insurance showing their own workers' comp policy.
  4. Check that each certificate's policy dates cover the whole time the sub worked for you, not only the day you hired them.
  5. Renewal certificates for any sub whose policy renewed during the year.
  6. Notes on any sub who had no coverage, and why, so you're not surprised by the bill.

NYSIF's advice is to keep a record of all subcontractor coverage documents you obtain during your policy period. A tracking file you keep up to date all year is far easier than rebuilding it the week of the audit. Our free COI tracking spreadsheet has a row per policy for exactly this.

Do 1099 subcontractors need their own workers' comp?

It depends on your state and on the work. Your workers' comp auditor will still ask for proof. Without a current certificate showing the sub's own coverage, what you paid them can be counted as your payroll. A sole owner with no employees may be exempt in some states, so ask your broker what your state accepts.

How do I avoid an audit surcharge next year?

Collect a certificate showing workers' comp before every sub starts work. Check that the policy dates cover the job. Request renewal certificates before each policy expires, and keep every certificate in one place. At audit time you hand over one complete file, and the auditor has no reason to add those payments to your payroll.

  1. Ask for the certificate before site access. Our COI request email template has the wording.
  2. Check each certificate when it arrives. Our guide to verifying a certificate of insurance walks through it field by field.
  3. Track every expiration date and chase renewals about 30 days ahead. See our guide to subcontractor insurance tracking.
  4. Keep the sub off site if the certificate lapses, until a new one arrives.

What else do contractors ask about workers' comp audits?

Contractors also ask how to fix a charge after the audit and what to do when a sub says the policy renewed but sends nothing. Workers' comp rules vary by state and by carrier. Use these answers as a starting point, then confirm the details with your broker before the audit.

Can I reduce an audit charge for a subcontractor I could not document?

Sometimes. If the sub did have workers' comp and you simply had no certificate, ask their insurance agent for a certificate covering the dates they worked and send it to the auditor. If the sub truly had no coverage, the charge usually stands. Ask your broker how your carrier handles disputes.

What if a subcontractor says the policy renewed but sends nothing?

Thank them and ask for the certificate anyway. A verbal yes isn't proof of coverage. Ask the agent listed as producer on the old certificate to send the renewal directly. Until it arrives, record the policy as expired and treat the sub as uninsured for site access.

Can I accept an expired COI?

No. An expired certificate proves nothing about current coverage. Require an updated certificate showing the renewed policy term before the sub continues work.
